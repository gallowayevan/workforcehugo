## Introduction
North Carolina is expected to face a shortage of more than [5,000 licensed practical nurses](https://ncnursecast.unc.edu/key-findings/) (LPNs) by 2033 with all regions of the state facing shortfalls. Nursing home, extended care, and assisted living facilities will be greatly affected, with LPN job openings projected to outnumber available LPNs by two to one.

LPNs provide patient care under the supervision of a registered nurse (RN) or other qualified healthcare [clinician](https://www4.ncleg.net/enactedlegislation/statutes/html/bysection/chapter_90/gs_90-171.20.html). Their role may include discussing health concerns with patients, reporting concerns to supervising clinicians, monitoring vital signs, changing bandages, assisting with bathing and dressing, recording patient status and the care provided, maintaining health records, etc. LPNs work in a variety of healthcare settings, with the largest portion working in long-term care (LTC) settings.

[Shortages of registered nurses in the wake of the COVID-19](https://ncnursecast.unc.edu/key-findings/) pandemic have caused healthcare organizations to explore new staffing models. A [2025 analysis](https://www.nursingoutlook.org/article/S0029-6554(25)00092-2/abstract) found a significant increase in the LPN employment in hospitals in recent years. If this trend continues, it will likely affect the availability of the LPNs to practice in LTC settings, which face increased demand due to NC’s aging population. This will in turn affect hospitals that may struggle to discharge patients requiring care in LTC settings. 

Addressing LPN shortages requires a multi-tiered approach. Strategies include increasing the number of graduates from LPN programs in the state, retaining existing LPNs in the workforce, and reengaging LPNs who have left practice. This report examines where North Carolina's LPN workforce is growing, where it is declining, and what these trends mean for employers, educators, workforce planners, and policymakers seeking to ensure adequate staffing across the continuum of care.

<div class="section-header">
  <h2 class="title is-2">The LPN Workforce Has Rebounded in Recent Years But Remains Smaller than a Decade Ago</h2>
</div>

In 2025, there were 17,765 LPNs in active practice in North Carolina (**Figure 1**). While the peak in 2018 is an artifact of a change in nursing data collection nationally, the decline in the number of LPNs in 2020, 2021, and 2022 is likely due to the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. To return to pre-pandemic staffing levels of 2019 would require more than 600 LPNs in the NC workforce.

<div class="section-header">
  <h2 class="title is-2">Rural Counties Continue to Have More LPNs Per Capita, but the Gap Is Narrowing</h2>
</div>

North Carolina’s health workforce is concentrated in metropolitan (urban) areas. However, the LPN workforce is an exception. The supply of LPNs has historically been ***higher*** in rural (non-metropolitan) counties compared to urban counties (**Figure 3**). The gap has been narrowing over time, particularly after the pandemic years. In 2025, there were 16.9 LPNs per 10,000 population in rural counties, relative to 15.8 per 10,000 population in urban counties.

  <h2 class="title is-2">One in Three LPNs Work in Long-Term Care Settings</h2>

The second most common employment setting for LPNs (21.9% in 2025, n=3,897) in 2025 was “other settings.” In this analysis, “other settings” includes correctional facilities, public health settings, occupational health settings, insurance claims & benefits, academic settings, school health service, community health settings, dialysis centers, mental health, and other settings.

<div class="section-header">
  <h2 class="title is-2">The Pandemic Accelerated Shifts in LPN Employment Settings</h2>
</div>

LPN employment trends shifted during the pandemic (**Table 2**). The number of LPNs in nursing home, extended care, and assisted living facilities fell by 1,097 LPNs (16.6%) between 2016 and 2025. More than half of this decline occurred between 2020 and 2022 when employment decreased by 580 LPNs (-9.6%). 

The number of LPNs working in hospital settings declined between 2016 and 2020 (n=-129; -10.2%) but after the peak of the pandemic between 2022 and 2025, hospital-employed LPNs grew by 33.5% (n=+383). LPN employment in ambulatory care grew prior to the pandemic by 42.4% (n=+612), however, that growth dropped off sharply (-17.7%; n=-363) during the pandemic years and then increased modestly between 2022 and 2025 (4.1%; n=+70). LPN employment in home health care and hospice settings increased from 2016 until 2020 by 390 LPNs (17.9%) but that growth has also slowed since 2020.

<h2 class="title is-2">LPN Employment in Long-Term Care Settings Has Declined, Especially in Rural Counties</h2>
</div>

Changes in LPN employment differed substantially between urban and rural counties, particularly in LTC settings. (**Table 3**). Over the past decade, LPN employment in nursing home, extended care, and assisted living settings fell by 29.5% (n=-515) in rural counties compared with a decline of 12.0% (n=-582) in urban counties. By contrast, hospital employment increased at similar rates in urban and rural counties, growing 21.0% (n=+202) and 20.5% (n=+61), respectively. Similarly, employment in ambulatory care settings increased by 22.0% (n=+263) in urban counties and 22.8% (n=+56) in rural counties. Overall, the number of active-practice LPNs increased 2.9% (n=+398) in metropolitan counties while declining 17.7% (n=-814) in nonmetropolitan counties.

<div class="section-header">
<h2 class="title is-2">Newly Licensed NC-Educated LPNs Were More Likely to Work in Hospitals than Were Newly Licensed LPNs from Out-of-State</h2>
</div>

To determine where newly licensed LPNs are getting jobs, we examined the practice setting of LPNs who were initially licensed in 2023. Not all LPNs in this cohort are recent graduates; some are new to NC and were previously practicing in another state. Of the 1,055 LPNs newly licensed in North Carolina in 2023 and practicing in 2025, 68.2% (n=720) were educated in North Carolina and 31.8% (n=335) were educated outside the state (**Table 5**). The largest employment setting for newly licensed LPNs was nursing homes, extended care, and assisted living facilities, which employed 35.5% (n=375) of newly licensed LPNs. Hospitals were the second-largest employment setting, employing 20.3% (n=214) of newly licensed LPNs, followed by other settings (18.1%, n=191) and home health and hospice (13.9%, n=147).

Although graduates of North Carolina programs represented 68.2% of newly licensed LPNs overall, they accounted for 85.0% (n=182 of 214) of newly licensed LPNs employed in hospitals.

More than 1 in 10 (11.0%, n=1,943) of active practice LPNs in 2024 were not in practice in 2025. Of those LPNs who were inactive, 31.1% (n=606/1943) remained in the NC workforce and were licensed as RNs. Nearly one-quarter of inactive LPNs (24.5%, n=476) had their license placed on inactive status or had relocated to another compact state. Of the remainder, 16% (n=316) left employment in nursing, 5.8% (n=112) reported being unemployed, and 4.7% (n=91) retired.

## Conclusion 

Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs) play a critical role in meeting the health care needs of North Carolinians, particularly those receiving care in nursing homes, extended care facilities, and assisted living settings. While the number of new LPNs entering the workforce from North Carolina educational programs has increased in recent years, overall workforce growth has not kept pace with the state's population growth. The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on the LPN workforce, with the number of active LPNs declining between 2020 and 2022. Although the workforce has partially recovered, North Carolina would need more than 600 additional LPNs to return to its pre-pandemic staffing level of 2019.

Long-term care settings continue to employ approximately one-third of North Carolina's LPN workforce, but employment in these settings has steadily declined over the past decade. This trend is particularly concerning given the state's aging population and growing demand for long-term care services. The decline has been especially pronounced in rural areas, where the number of LPNs working in nursing homes, extended care facilities, and assisted living facilities fell nearly 30% between 2016 and 2025. At the same time, LPNs are increasingly employed in hospitals and ambulatory care settings. These shifts may create challenges not only for long-term care providers seeking to recruit and retain staff, but also for hospitals that depend on a well-functioning long-term care system to facilitate timely patient discharge and transitions of care.

The educational pipeline offers some encouraging signs. Enrollment in North Carolina LPN programs has increased substantially in recent years, and the number of graduates taking the NCLEX-PN examination has also grown. However, the data suggest that many newly licensed LPNs educated in North Carolina are entering hospital practice, while LPNs educated outside the state are more likely to work in long-term care settings. As a result, increasing the number of graduates alone may not be sufficient to address workforce shortages in nursing homes, extended care facilities, and assisted living settings.

Addressing LPN workforce challenges will require a combination of strategies focused on increasing workforce supply and retention. Continued investment in LPN education programs should be paired with efforts to improve recruitment and retention in long-term care settings, where working conditions can be demanding and compensation often lags behind other health care sectors. Career mobility pathways are also an important component of the workforce sustainability. Nearly one-third of LPNs who left active LPN practice between 2024 and 2025 remained in nursing by becoming registered nurses. Strengthening pathways that support Certified Nursing Assistants in becoming LPNs, while also creating incentives for LPNs to practice and remain in long-term care, may help ensure an adequate workforce to meet North Carolina's future health care needs.
